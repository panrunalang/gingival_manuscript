# Point-by-point response to Reviewer 2

Dear Editor and Reviewers,

Thank you for the careful review of the manuscript entitled “Preoperative Information-Seeking Behavior and Perioperative Experiences in Patients Undergoing Periodontal Mucogingival Surgery: A Single-Centre Cross-Sectional Survey.” The manuscript has been revised to clarify the study measures, complete the clinical and descriptive reporting, strengthen the statistical analyses, and qualify the interpretation of the findings.

Each comment is reproduced below, followed by a response and the relevant revision locations. Line numbers refer to the continuous line numbering in the final highlighted Word manuscript (manuscript-highlighted version.docx), beginning with the title. Page numbers refer to the same document. Because Word does not number text within tables, table locations are identified by table number and page; supplementary materials are identified by file designation and item or table number.

Responses to the comments:

## Background and general questions

### Comment 1

The rationale and value of this study should be clearer. Why is this focus important, and what is its clinical relevance? The emphasis at the end of the Introduction appears somewhat overstated and may not be supported by sufficient evidence.

Response: Thank you for requesting a clearer rationale and clinical context. The Introduction retains the background on GR-related sensitivity, root-surface lesions, plaque control, and aesthetics. The question now focuses on how patients who underwent mucogingival surgery obtained preoperative information, understood surgery, and reported perioperative experiences. Understanding the need for surgery, the procedure, risks, costs, and expected outcomes is relevant to informed decisions and provides a clinical rationale for describing information seeking and communication needs.

The final paragraph of the Introduction no longer claims that the study can improve treatment acceptance or outcomes. Instead, the findings may identify topics for preoperative communication and health education; their effects on surgical acceptance or treatment outcomes were not evaluated. Clinical implications in Conclusions are likewise restricted to questions requiring prospective evaluation.

Revision location: Introduction, lines 49–80; Conclusions, lines 425–434.

### Comment 2

Previous studies have examined disease awareness and treatment acceptance among patients with gingival recession, as well as preoperative anxiety and postoperative experiences in mucogingival surgery. Introduction and Discussion should explain more clearly what new knowledge this study adds to the existing literature. This is currently vague.

Response: Thank you for requesting a contribution grounded in existing evidence. The Introduction now describes previous GR-recognition studies and distinguishes disease awareness from preoperative information seeking and surgical understanding in this survey. Prior evidence is no longer presented as absent. The Discussion also cites associations between anxiety and postoperative symptoms in third-molar surgery as background, without treating similar findings across surgical settings as first discoveries.

The incremental contribution is defined as a focus on GR patients who actually underwent mucogingival surgery, jointly describing preoperative information channels, procedure-specific understanding, and perioperative experiences in the same population, and exploring factors associated with information sources and ordinal associations between experiences. Claims of first reporting based on the retrospective design have been removed. No new causal mechanism is claimed.

Revision location: Introduction, lines 59–80; Discussion, lines 280–288 and 348–354.

## Methods and Results

### Comment 1

What was the interval between surgery and questionnaire completion? The retrospective questionnaire design may introduce recall bias, especially for preoperative anxiety and information seeking. Any causal interpretation should also be avoided.

Response: Thank you for raising survey timing and recall bias. The Methods section clarifies that questionnaires were completed at each patient’s scheduled six-month postoperative follow-up. July 2022 to December 2025 was the screening window for surgical cases, rather than a period recalled by all patients at one survey date after its end. The abstract and Methods consistently describe a cross-sectional questionnaire survey, with postoperative retrospective assessment of preoperative anxiety and information seeking.

Recall bias is identified as a major limitation. Postoperative experiences may influence recalled preoperative states, and a common scheduled follow-up point limits variation in recall periods without eliminating bias. The manuscript does not provide individual actual intervals or interval-stratified analyses, so it cannot claim that interval differences were statistically examined. The Discussion and Conclusions describe exploratory associations. Future-treatment responses represent intentions, rather than actual acceptance or avoidance.

Revision location: Abstract, lines 25–31; Methods, lines 83–94 and 108–115; Discussion, lines 372–374, 386–406, and 419–423; Conclusions, lines 425–434.

### Comment 2

Since all participants had already undergone mucogingival surgery, this study cannot directly assess factors associated with accepting or refusing surgery, correct? Statements about surgical acceptance or avoidance therefore require more cautious revision.

Response: Thank you for identifying this limitation of the study population. This assessment is correct: all participants had undergone mucogingival surgery, and there was no comparison group that refused surgery or selected another treatment. The study cannot directly identify determinants of surgical acceptance or refusal. The original wording of the administered decision-making items is retained in Supplementary Material S3; the revision changes their interpretation, rather than the questions participants answered.

Relevant items are now interpreted as self-reported influences on decisions among patients who underwent surgery; future treatment choices are stated intentions. Results, table notes, and Conclusions use expressions such as “reported decision-making influences.” The Discussion states that surgical avoidance was not measured, and the Introduction clarifies that effects on surgical acceptance were not evaluated. The limitations and Conclusions sections explain that these data cannot distinguish acceptors from refusers or establish that postoperative symptoms cause actual avoidance.

Revision location: Introduction, lines 72–80; Methods, lines 155–169 and 216–225; Results, lines 237–245 and 266–278; Discussion, lines 372–374 and 392–396; Conclusions, lines 425–434; Table 2 note, lines 622–626 (p. 30).

### Comment 3

How is “subjective awareness” defined? Why can active seeking of professional information serve as a proxy for awareness?

Response: Thank you for questioning this definition and proxy measurement. Selecting a professional information source does not establish actual knowledge or provide a validated indicator of adequate awareness. The proxy interpretation has been removed. The regression outcome is now explicitly named professional information seeking, defined as selecting the attending doctor or nurse in the single-choice information-source question, compared with all other responses.

Understanding of the surgical procedure and knowledge of initial periodontal therapy are reported separately as descriptive items of procedure-specific understanding, rather than combined with information sources as one construct. These knowledge items have not undergone composite-scale validation and show ceiling effects; no total score is constructed. The single-choice information-source item also cannot capture multiple channels used by one patient, or distinguish routine clinical explanations from information actively requested by the patient. The limitation concerning multiple information channels is stated in the manuscript. The title, abstract, objectives, and conclusions have been revised consistently.

Revision location: Title, lines 1–5; Abstract, lines 21–45; Introduction, lines 72–80; Methods, lines 151–169 and 180–183; Discussion, lines 407–414; Conclusions, lines 425–434; Table 2, pp. 29–30.

### Comment 4

How were levels of preoperative anxiety and postoperative reactions defined? Did patients select the levels themselves, or did researchers classify them using specific criteria?

Response: Thank you for requesting clarification. The Methods section states that patients selected the predefined response best matching their recollection, without researcher grading using additional criteria. Preoperative anxiety had four levels: none; mild, with tension, unease, worry without a clear cause, or panic; moderate, with persistent tension, unease, worry, palpitations, or a pounding heartbeat; and severe, with persistent tension, unease, and worry accompanied by somatic symptoms such as headache, dizziness, abdominal pain, or diarrhea. Postoperative reactions had three levels: no particular discomfort; mild bleeding, pain, or swelling; and marked pain or swelling requiring oral analgesics for relief.

These descriptions are questionnaire response options, rather than validated anxiety diagnostic criteria or clinical symptom-severity standards. The Table 2 note also states that patients selected their grades. The limitations section acknowledges the self-developed single anxiety item and uncertainty in measurement precision. Validated psychometric scales are recommended for future studies.

Revision location: Methods, lines 170–178; Discussion, lines 410–414 and 419–423; Table 2 note, lines 622–626 (p. 30); Supplementary Material S3, items 11 and 15.

### Comment 5

How was the sample size determined? Was any sample-size estimation or statistical power calculation performed before the study?

Response: Thank you for asking about sample-size determination. The Methods section states that all periodontal surgical cases from July 2022 to December 2025 were screened, and all 72 eligible mucogingival surgery patients within that window were invited. All 72 submitted questionnaires, six were excluded after quality screening, and 66 were analyzed. Sample size was determined by eligible patient numbers and quality screening, without a priori sample-size estimation or statistical power calculation.

The limitations section states that adequate power for each association analysis was not established in advance. Results failing to reach P < 0.05 should not be used to exclude real associations. Model sample sizes, event counts, and EPV are reported, and Firth sensitivity analyses have been added. These measures do not replace prospective sample-size planning or eliminate uncertainty from a small sample and wide CIs. The findings are exploratory and require validation in larger samples.

Revision location: Methods, lines 86–98, 129–137, and 212–215; Results, lines 253–264; Discussion, lines 396–406; Figure 1 caption, lines 586–590 (p. 27); Supplementary Material S4, Table S3.

## Discussion and Conclusion

### Comment 6

Since the authors acknowledge that causality cannot be established, Conclusions should be further tempered. Some conclusions still appear overstated.

Response: Thank you for recommending more cautious conclusions. The Conclusions section now describes exploratory associations among patients who underwent mucogingival surgery. Recommendations for routine psychological assessment and counseling have been removed, as has the claim that the study demonstrates interruption of a causal cycle involving anxiety, negative experiences, and surgical avoidance. The abstract uses the same interpretive scope, stating that implications for preoperative communication require prospective evaluation.

The Discussion clarifies that effects of psychological assessment, counseling, or systemic health assessment were not evaluated, and actual surgical avoidance was not measured. The limitations section addresses the absence of patients who refused surgery, intentions rather than actual future choices, residual confounding and multiple comparisons, and inability to establish causality. Future research is proposed as questions for testing, rather than as intervention effects established by these data.

Revision location: Abstract Conclusions, lines 41–45; Discussion, lines 334–374, 386–406, and 419–423; Conclusions, lines 425–434.
