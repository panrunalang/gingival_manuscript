# Point-by-point response to Reviewer 1

Dear Editor and Reviewers,

Thank you for the careful review of the manuscript entitled “Preoperative Information-Seeking Behavior and Perioperative Experiences in Patients Undergoing Periodontal Mucogingival Surgery: A Single-Centre Cross-Sectional Survey.” The manuscript has been revised to clarify the study measures, complete the clinical and descriptive reporting, strengthen the statistical analyses, and qualify the interpretation of the findings.

Each comment is reproduced below, followed by a response and the relevant revision locations. Line numbers refer to the continuous line numbering in the final highlighted Word manuscript (manuscript-highlighted version.docx), beginning with the title. Page numbers refer to the same document. Because Word does not number text within tables, table locations are identified by table number and page; supplementary materials are identified by file designation and item or table number.

Responses to the comments:

## Major comments

### Comment 1

Construct validity of “awareness” is the central issue. Obtaining information through professional channels is used as an operational proxy for awareness, but an information source is not equivalent to actual knowledge or awareness. This definition is also somewhat circular: a patient who receives an explanation from a clinician is classified as aware because of the channel itself. The questionnaire already includes knowledge-related items, including understanding of the surgical procedure (96.97% partially or fully understood) and knowledge of the need for initial periodontal therapy (98.48%). These data were collected but not used in modeling.

Either (a) develop and validate a composite knowledge score from the relevant items in Part II and use it as the outcome, or (b) retain the outcome but consistently name it “professional information-seeking behaviour,” remove its description as a proxy for awareness, and revise the title, abstract, and conclusions. The Introduction states that awareness remains inadequate, whereas the results show procedure-related awareness in 97%–98% of patients.

Response: Thank you for identifying this central issue in the definition of the study constructs. An information source does not establish the knowledge a patient actually possesses or whether awareness is adequate. The revised manuscript follows the second option. The regression outcome is explicitly defined as professional information seeking: selecting the attending doctor or nurse in the single-choice question about preoperative information sources. Its interpretation as a proxy for awareness has been removed. The title, abstract, objectives, results, and conclusions now focus on information-seeking behavior and procedure-specific understanding.

Understanding of the surgical procedure and knowledge of initial periodontal therapy are reported as separate descriptive items, without a combined knowledge score. Neither item has undergone scale validation, and both show ceiling effects; they cannot support a validated composite knowledge outcome. The Introduction also distinguishes disease recognition in previous studies from surgical information and understanding in this survey, without assuming that the participants had inadequate awareness.

Revision location: Title, lines 1–5; Abstract, lines 21–45; Introduction, lines 59–80; Methods, lines 151–169 and 180–183; Results, lines 237–245; Discussion, lines 407–414; Conclusions, lines 425–434; Table 2, pp. 29–30.

### Comment 2

The information-source item is a single-choice question. Q10 in the Supporting Information requires one answer from Internet, people around me, attending doctor or nurse, or did not learn. The reported proportions of 93.94% actively seeking information and 43.94% using professional sources are therefore constrained by forced single-choice measurement. A patient who received a clinician’s explanation and also searched online can only be assigned to one channel. Since the outcomes of both regression models derive from this item, this is an important measurement limitation. Clarify it in Methods and Limitations, and temper the inference that more than half relied on the Internet, family or friends, or no information, which the item cannot support.

Response: Thank you for highlighting this limitation. The Methods section now states that the questionnaire contained 20 single-choice items. The information-source question allowed only one response: the Internet, people around the patient, the attending doctor or nurse, or no information obtained. The regression outcome therefore represents the selected channel, rather than any previous exposure to professional information. The limitations section explains that patients may use several channels and that someone who consulted a healthcare professional may nevertheless select another answer.

The proportions are now described as patients reporting that they had learned about the procedure before surgery and patients selecting healthcare professionals as their information source. Other answers are no longer interpreted as evidence that patients received no professional explanation or relied exclusively on nonprofessional sources. Verification against the source data identified 30 patients (45.45%) selecting professional sources, replacing 29 (43.94%). The proportion of 93.94% represents selection of any information channel, rather than an independently measured rate of active information seeking. The item also does not distinguish a clinician’s routine explanation from information actively requested by the patient.

Revision location: Methods, lines 165–169 and 180–183; Results, lines 237–240; Discussion, lines 289–300 and 407–409; Table 3 note, lines 629–639 (p. 32); Supplementary Material S3, item 10.

### Comment 4

Clinical characteristics of the mucogingival procedures are entirely absent, including the number and location of treated recessions, recession depth, keratinized tissue width, technique (CAF, CAF+CTG, tunnel, CAF+collagen matrix, etc.), palatal donor site and its size, adjunctive medication, and root coverage. Without at least technique and donor-site information, postoperative reactions are difficult to interpret, because discomfort after free gingival or connective tissue grafting differs from procedures using matrices or no graft. Add a clinical characteristics table and, if sample size permits, consider technique and donor-site status as covariates. If unavailable in the records, acknowledge this as a major limitation.

Response: Thank you for noting the clinical information needed to interpret postoperative experiences. A new Table 1 reports the number and jaw location of treated teeth, recession depth, keratinized tissue width, surgical technique, palatal harvesting method, graft dimensions, postoperative medication, and root coverage at six months. The 66 patients had 98 treated teeth. All underwent the envelope technique with a palatal connective tissue graft (CTG), harvested using a single-incision technique. The table note explains that, for multiple adjacent treated teeth, the relevant measurements were recorded as the minimum value per patient, and that graft dimensions do not represent palatal wound area.

Surgical technique and the presence of a palatal donor site were identical across patients, providing no variation for estimating between-group differences. Neither was therefore included as a covariate. The added information provides clinical context but does not permit comparison of different techniques or donor-site conditions. The absence of adjustment for other covariates in the three experience-related association analyses is also explicitly acknowledged.

### Comment 6

Retrospective design and recall bias require attention. Surgery occurred between July 2022 and December 2025, but preoperative anxiety and information seeking were assessed retrospectively. The surgery-to-questionnaire interval is not reported and may exceed three years for the earliest patients, threatening the validity of these core exposures. Report its distribution, examine differences by interval, and discuss recall bias as a major limitation. Also reconcile “retrospective questionnaire survey” in Methods with “cross-sectional design” in Limitations, and identify the design in the abstract.

Response: Thank you for raising this concern. The manuscript now clarifies that July 2022 to December 2025 was the screening window for surgical cases. Each questionnaire was completed at the patient’s scheduled six-month postoperative follow-up, rather than at a single survey date covering the whole surgical period. The interval between the earliest surgery and the end of screening therefore cannot establish a recall period exceeding three years.

The design is consistently described as a cross-sectional questionnaire survey, with retrospective assessment of preoperative anxiety and information seeking, including in the abstract. Recall bias is a major limitation: a common scheduled follow-up point limits variation in recall periods but cannot exclude effects of postoperative experiences on recalled preoperative states. Individual actual intervals and interval-stratified analyses are not available in the report. Consequently, scheduled six-month follow-up does not mean that all actual intervals were identical, and interval effects have not been tested.

Revision location: Abstract, lines 25–31; Methods, lines 83–94 and 108–115; Discussion, lines 386–392 and 419–423.

### Comment 7

The third-molar surgery study is cited as background evidence from another oral surgical setting. Its anxiety measures are not treated as directly comparable to, or convertible into, this survey’s four-level item. Validated psychometric scales are recommended for future studies. These data do not support a clinical diagnosis of anxiety based on the single-item response.

### Comment 8 (1)

The analyses ignore the ordering of categorical variables. Preoperative anxiety (none/mild/moderate), postoperative reactions (none/mild/marked), and influence on future surgical willingness (none/mild/moderate) are ordinal. Fisher–Freeman–Halton treats them as nominal and tests only general association. Consider linear-by-linear association or Cochran–Armitage-type trend tests, Kendall’s tau-b, or gamma with 95% CIs. Directional expressions such as “positively associated” require such analyses; the current exact test alone does not support them.

Response: Thank you for recommending analyses that reflect ordinal structure. The revised analyses use linear-by-linear association tests for the three trends and Goodman–Kruskal gamma with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for direction and strength, replacing the Fisher–Freeman–Halton analyses of general association. Grades are ordered from no anxiety, discomfort, or influence to increasing severity; psychological burden is coded as no/yes. Gamma CIs are based on 10,000 patient-level bootstrap samples. The reported P values come from the linear-by-linear association tests, rather than the bootstrap CIs.

Gamma was 0.611 for preoperative anxiety and postoperative reactions (95% CI 0.213–0.865; P = 0.009), 0.783 for preoperative anxiety and the influence of psychological burden on decision-making (95% CI 0.477–0.956; P < 0.001), and 0.740 for postoperative reactions and influence on future surgical decisions (95% CI 0.436–0.945; P < 0.001). Directional statements now rely on ordinal effect estimates, while retaining exploratory, noncausal interpretation.

Revision location: Methods, lines 216–225; Results, lines 266–278; Discussion, lines 348–374; Figure 3 caption, lines 599–605 (p. 28).

The limitations section further emphasizes small sample size and chance findings.

### Comment 8 (3)

Since age (OR ≈ 0.88) and sex (OR ≈ 1.12) have little apparent effect, a reduction of about 32% is difficult to explain. Response: Thank you for checking these estimates. Verification against the source data corrected information-source counts and several demographic category counts. Of the 66 patients, 30 selected the attending doctor or nurse. Outcome definition, original category coding, adjustment variables, and missing-response handling are now explicit; descriptive tables and regression estimates have been updated together.

Education retains four original categories, scored 0–3 to estimate the trend per one-category increase. The crude OR is 1.57 (95% CI 0.85–2.89; P = 0.151), and the age- and sex-adjusted OR is 2.50 (95% CI 1.11–5.63; P = 0.026). The Firth sensitivity estimate is 2.24 (95% CI 1.11–5.20; P = 0.022). These estimates concern a trend across original categories, a different estimand from the initial binary education comparison. They replace OR = 3.21, and the change from the original crude to adjusted binary OR cannot be compared directly with the difference between these revised trend estimates.

Fourteen income responses and one marital-status response were “prefer not to answer.” These patients were excluded only from the corresponding primary models, retained in other analyses, and not numerically imputed. Crude and adjusted associations are reported separately. Their difference is not attributed to the P values for age or sex, and education is not interpreted as an independent or causal determinant.

### Comment 8 (4)

Explain model construction. Age and sex each have univariate P = 1.000 but enter the model because of “clinical relevance”; the rationale should be specified in advance. There are 29 events and three predictors, EPV ≈ 9.7. Report EPV explicitly and consider Firth penalized logistic regression as a sensitivity analysis. “Estimated events per variable were insufficient” in Limitations is imprecise.

Response: Thank you for the recommendations on adjustment, small-sample estimation, and events per variable (EPV). A consistent limited-adjustment approach is now applied to each target factor. The seven factors other than age and sex are entered into separate models, each adjusted for original age categories and sex; age and sex are mutually adjusted. Variables are not selected using univariate P values. The Methods section explains the use of age and sex as basic demographic adjustment factors and cites literature on avoiding univariate significance screening and on age- and sex-related information-seeking patterns. These are revised analyses, and the limitations section states that they were not documented in a prespecified analysis plan.

EPV is calculated using the number of predictor parameters, excluding the intercept and counting categorical indicators separately. The education model has 30 events and four predictor parameters, EPV = 7.50. EPV ranges from 4.29 to 10.00 across the primary adjusted models; model-specific values appear in Supplementary Material S4. All adjusted models also undergo Firth penalized logistic regression, with profile penalized-likelihood 95% CIs and penalized likelihood-ratio tests. The Firth education-trend OR is 2.24 (95% CI 1.11–5.20; P = 0.022).

Sparse marital-status and insurance categories produce separation in ordinary regression; finite Firth estimates are reported. The commercial-insurance comparison includes only two patients and has a wide CI despite P = 0.042; the overall insurance test gives P = 0.317. This uncertainty is explicit. Firth estimation cannot eliminate small-sample limitations or residual confounding, and the target factors are not mutually adjusted.

### Comment 8 (5)

Categories were collapsed after data collection: four education levels, five income levels, four age groups, and time since first dental attendance were dichotomized. Explain whether the cutoffs were prespecified and provide a rationale for each.

Response: Thank you for asking about category merging and prespecification. Documentation cannot establish that the original binary cutoffs were prespecified. The revised analyses therefore remove these merges and retain the questionnaire’s original categories for age, education, income, and time since first dental treatment. Original nominal categories for marital status and insurance are also retained and displayed with counts in Table 3.

Education is scored 0–3 from below junior college to postgraduate education. Income and time since first dental treatment are scored in ascending original-category order, 0–4 and 0–3, respectively. ORs represent a one-category increase. These scores express ordering, not equally spaced years of education, income amounts, or treatment intervals, and assume equal log-odds increments between adjacent categories. Age is modeled using categorical indicators. The Methods and limitations sections explain these assumptions and the exploratory nature of the analyses. No new outcome-dependent binary cutoff has been introduced.
